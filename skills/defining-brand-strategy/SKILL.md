# Defining Brand Strategy

## Core principle
Brand strategy is a decision system, not a moodboard. Convert evidence about the audience, category, offer, and business into constraints that make later naming, visual, and verbal choices easier to judge.

## Strategy workflow
1. Establish the assignment. State what is changing, why now, which business or product outcome matters, and what is outside scope.
2. Separate evidence from assumption. Capture customer language, category expectations, stakeholder beliefs, product facts, and unanswered questions without flattening them into one narrative.
3. Define the audience by situation and need. Prefer the problem, motivation, alternative, and buying or adoption context over broad demographic personas.
4. Map the competitive frame. Identify the category users think they are choosing within, the conventions competitors repeat, and the spaces where meaningful distinction is possible.
5. Define the value proposition and proof. State the useful promise, why it matters to the audience, and what evidence makes the promise credible.
6. Write the positioning. Clarify who the brand is for, the frame of reference, the meaningful difference, and the reason to believe it.
7. Define personality as behavioral attributes. For each attribute, state what it looks like in decisions and what would violate it. Avoid adjective piles such as “bold, modern, human” without operational meaning.
8. Create brand principles and guardrails. Record what the brand should consistently express, what it must avoid, and which tensions it intentionally balances.
9. Turn the strategy into evaluation criteria for naming, visual identity, and messaging. Later concepts should be judged against the strategy rather than stakeholder taste alone.

## Strategy quality checks
A useful strategy should:
- distinguish facts, assumptions, and hypotheses;
- describe a real audience tension or job, not a fictional archetype;
- make the brand meaningfully different without claiming uniqueness that cannot be proved;
- connect differentiation to product or organizational reality;
- give creative teams enough constraint to reject plausible but off-strategy work;
- remain short enough to be used in decisions.

## Avoid
- starting with logos, colors, archetypes, or taglines before the positioning problem is understood;
- treating a competitor moodboard as strategy;
- inventing purpose language unsupported by the organization;
- equating “premium”, “innovative”, or “authentic” with differentiation;
- forcing consensus by making the strategy so broad that every creative direction fits.

## Verification
Before handing off to downstream brand work, test the strategy against three decisions: one naming choice, one visual direction, and one messaging choice. If the strategy cannot explain why one option fits better than another, its constraints are still too weak.
