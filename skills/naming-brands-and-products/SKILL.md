# Naming Brands and Products

## Core principle
Naming is strategic selection under constraints, not a contest for the cleverest word. Generate broadly, evaluate consistently, and separate creative preference from linguistic, operational, and legal risk.

## Naming workflow
1. Write a naming brief from the brand strategy: audience, offer, desired associations, personality, category frame, languages/markets, naming architecture, and hard constraints.
2. Define evaluation criteria before generating candidates. Typical criteria include strategic fit, distinctiveness, memorability, pronunciation, spelling, extension potential, and avoidable negative associations.
3. Build several naming territories instead of one word list. Territories may use descriptive, suggestive, metaphorical, invented, founder/place, compound, or other structures when they fit the brief.
4. Generate breadth inside each territory. Do not evaluate every idea while generating; premature filtering collapses diversity.
5. Run a linguistic screen. Read candidates aloud, test likely pronunciations and misspellings, inspect meanings and unintended associations in relevant languages, and flag confusing similarity to category terms or competitors.
6. Test names in realistic context: spoken introduction, URL/search result, product UI, endorsement line, sub-brand extension, and support conversation.
7. Run preliminary availability checks for obvious conflicts. Search target markets and relevant goods/services using authoritative trademark databases where available. Treat domains and social handles as operational signals, not proof of trademark availability.
8. Score the shortlist against the criteria and document trade-offs. Prefer a small set of candidates with clear rationale over dozens of unranked options.
9. Escalate legal clearance when the name will carry material commercial value. A naming exercise or database search is not legal advice and cannot guarantee registrability or freedom to use.

## Red flags
Reject or downgrade a candidate when it:
- needs a long explanation before it makes sense;
- is only distinctive through unusual spelling that harms recall or pronunciation;
- creates a misleading category or product promise;
- has obvious conflicts in a target market;
- works only in a design mockup but fails when spoken or typed;
- blocks likely future extensions without a strategic reason.

## Verification
For each finalist, record the territory, strategic rationale, pronunciation, likely spelling errors, linguistic concerns, preliminary trademark-search notes, digital-operational notes, and unresolved risks. The shortlist should make the remaining decision explicit rather than hide uncertainty.
